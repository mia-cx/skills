# Writing

Part of [gtm](../gtm/SKILL.md): its copy sheet and boundaries apply here.

Write posts and short copy that get read and acted on: one reader, one action, a first line that earns the second, a shape a phone skimmer can follow, and an ask at the end. Sentence-level style follows [talk-normal](../talk-normal/SKILL.md). For Mia, her voice follows [talk-like-mia](../talk-like-mia/SKILL.md), and where it conflicts with a formatting rule here (bullets, lists, length), talk-like-mia wins.

## 1. Plan the post

Write three lines above every draft, because once you know a topic it's hard to picture a reader who doesn't (the curse of knowledge, Chip and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*), and naming the reader first is the check:

```
Reader: <the one reader from the copy sheet's positioning, or the lead>
Action: <one of read, reply, share, follow, save, click, buy>
Takeaway: <one sentence the reader walks away with>
```

- **One idea per post.** One idea is easier to remember and pass on. Three ideas make three posts. How each platform's feed treats a post lives in its branch ([gtm-x](../gtm-x/SKILL.md), [gtm-linkedin](../gtm-linkedin/SKILL.md)).
- **Advanced idea, simple words.** Keep the concept intact and the vocabulary at a 6th–8th grade reading level (ages 11–14), because even domain experts prefer plain, scannable text (Hoa Loranger, NN/g).
- **Pass the magician test.** A layperson should find it interesting and an expert ten times more so. Copy written for a general audience still lands with experts, but copy written only for experts loses everyone else.
- **Tell the change from the user's side.** Find the moment things became different for someone, because people pull products into their lives to make progress (Christensen Institute, jobs to be done). The user is the main character and the product is what helped. When no real change exists yet, ask the user for one instead of writing a story without it.

Done when the three lines are filled in and every sentence of the draft serves them.

## 2. Write the body, then the hook

Write the body first, because the hook can only promise what the post delivers. Then write 5–10 hooks using different types, cut the weakest at once, refine the survivors until each line rolls into the next, and keep one. Hand the user the best two. Writing many headlines and keeping the best is John Caples's rule from decades of tested ads (*Tested Advertising Methods*); the 5–10 is a working default.

A hook's only job is to get the next line read. It opens a gap in what the reader knows that they want closed (George Loewenstein's information-gap account of curiosity) and makes it about them:

- Lead with the most interesting thing. If the best line sits at the bottom, move it to the top.
- Be specific: numbers, names and timeframes. "I hit 5.8M impressions in my first month" beats "I grew fast". Stop before the line gives the whole answer away: across 8,977 Upworthy headline tests, the more concrete headline won clicks when a test's headlines were vague, and lost when they were already concrete (Aubin Le Quéré and Matias 2025).
- Lead with self-interest (what the reader gets) or news, because they were Caples's strongest headlines. Add curiosity, novelty, emotion or identity on top; curiosity alone is "seldom enough" (Caples), and earlier experiments on curiosity-gap headlines split between helping, hurting and doing nothing (Aubin Le Quéré and Matias). "If you're a founder under 1K followers, this one change doubled my reach" is identity, then self-interest plus curiosity.

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Bold claim | "Copywriting is the most underrated skill on X." |
| Callout | "If you're a founder with under 1K followers, read this." |
| Curiosity gap (pair it with a benefit) | "The one change most founders miss that doubled my reach." |
| Clear outcome | "How to write posts like the top 1%." |
| Contrarian | "You need to post less." |
| Story | "It took me 8 years to realize this..." |
| Number or list | "7 copywriting rules I wish I knew 6 years ago." |
| Proof | "This post got 2M views. I analyzed why." |

Use only numbers the user gave you or the copy sheet holds. Place the hook where the format needs it:

```
Short post   → the hook is the post; line one stands alone
Thread       → hook plus a promise of what's coming ("Here are 7 lessons:")
Article      → title, subtitle and first paragraph each hook on their own
Video        → the first seconds: movement, on-screen text, spoken hook
Quote post   → the take adds tension or value to the original
Image        → the headline on the image, one idea, readable at phone size
```

## 3. Shape it for skimmers

Pick a shape before writing: problem → insight → takeaway; a list (hook, numbered points, closer); a graphic; a video; an article; a quote post. Put the point, the proof and the credibility in the opening lines, because scanners read the top and the left edge and drop off below (Kara Pernice, NN/g eyetracking).

Close on one of three: the takeaway in 1–2 lines, a question, or a tease of what's next.

Format for scanners on phones, because 79% of people scan before they read and concise, scannable text tested 124% more usable (Jakob Nielsen, NN/g), and about 60% of web page views worldwide now come from phones (StatCounter, October 2026):

- Keep paragraphs to 1–3 lines; four lines look like an essay on a phone.
- One thought per line. A sentence with two "and"s and a comma is probably two sentences.
- Use line breaks, numbers and arrows so a scanner picks up the point.
- Start each line with the words that carry the meaning.
- Use plain, exact words over hype ("doubled in a week", not "exploded"), because readers distrust promotional language.
- Say "you" more than "I".

Done when the draft has a shape, the proof in its opening lines, a deliberate last line, and no paragraph over three lines.

## 4. Ask for one thing

End on one low-effort ask: the action from step 1. A call to action is the prompt in BJ Fogg's behaviour model, where nothing happens without a prompt and a smaller step raises the odds. Match it to how well the reader knows the user, because small commitments lead to bigger ones (Cialdini, consistency) and asking a stranger for a sale is a big jump:

| Reader | Ask for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doesn't know the user yet | A follow or a share | "Send this to a friend who's building a brand." |
| Trusts them a bit | A reply, a save or a click | "Which one do you disagree with?" / "Full breakdown in the article below." |
| Ready | A sale, a DM or a sign-up | "DM me 'system' and I'll send you the details." |

The end isn't the only place for the ask. In long posts and articles, put one early too, because attention falls with every screen down (57% of viewing time goes to the first screen of a web page, Therese Fessenden, NN/g). Try asks that invite the reader in: their advice, or what they want covered next. One creator also posts the ask as a reply under their best-performing post; propose it as a test.

## Voice

Hooks and formats are shared; how the user sounds is much harder to copy, and a voice that stays the same lets readers tell at a glance that a post is theirs, the way a brand's consistent distinctive assets do (Jenni Romaniuk). Drafts sound like the user talks, hold their opinions, and use what actually happened to them. For anyone other than Mia, get a five-line voice card before drafting: their tone on NN/g's four scales (funny–serious, formal–casual, respectful–irreverent, enthusiastic–matter-of-fact), two opinions others in their field don't share, one true story, one quirk to keep. Store it in `.gtm/voice.md`. When the card says funny, warn that humour can cost trust (Kate Moran, NN/g) and test it on real readers.

AI drafts always look polished, and in tests with 4,600 people readers couldn't tell AI-written self-descriptions from human ones (Jakesch, Hancock and Naaman 2023), so polish says nothing about quality; the bar is whether the user would sign their name to it:

- Every draft holds lines a person would say out loud, zero jargon, and at least one number or example from the user's notes, because specifics are more believable than generalities (Caples).
- Invent no result, client name or number. Missing material becomes a question for the user.
- Before handing over, edit like a reader: mark the line where a reader would stop, and fix it or say so.

When the user publishes an edited version of your draft, compare the two. Once a week, propose the five edits they made most often as new lines for `.gtm/voice.md` (for Mia, as proposed changes to talk-like-mia), so the next drafts need less rewriting.
